## The choosing session

<!-- The six questions verbatim (they are stable kit content), the didactic notes
around them, and the expected output. Annex E maps each question to a workbook column. -->

{Six questions, asked in order, take the leadership team from {n_opportunities}
opportunities to a defensible starting set in one working session, choosing among
routes. The enablers are not on the menu: they proceed in wave order regardless, and
their gaps enter only as the gates that decide whether a route is open.}

[!box:The six questions]

1. Where does unstarted value concentrate? Rank functions by weighted mass, then ask
   each owning team what is genuinely already moving.
2. Is the path open or gated? Open routes can start now, and gated routes are not
   lesser; their first step is the gate.
3. If gated, what does moving the gate release? Prefer gates that release many
   opportunities.
4. How strong is the evidence underneath? Deep evidence supports commitment; thin
   evidence supports a verification step before a build.
5. What does it cost to try? Grade effort route by route and use it to order the
   starting set. Effort decides the sequence and should never decide worth: a hard
   route at the top of the discovery still outranks an easy one at the bottom.
6. What does the route assume, and who owns testing it? State the assumption, name an
   owner, and test it before funding the work.

[!/box]

{Before the session: owning teams re-confirm what is moving, since the evidence
describes {evidence_period}. Expected output: one gate move, a few open-route starts
where untouched value concentrates, one measurement move. Re-run quarterly, at any
change of structure or budget, and whenever an enabling gap closes.}

{The don'ts, adapted to the engagement. The stable six:}

1. Do not build a weighted composite score to rank the opportunities. The weights would
   be invented rather than evidenced, and the argument shifts from the work itself to
   the scoring formula.
2. Do not plan or grade effort for a whole stream at once. Commit route by route.
3. Do not ask this evidence to justify role-level decisions. It was gathered under a
   productivity scope and describes friction in processes; it says nothing about
   whether any role is needed.
4. Do not add new investment where work is already moving. Confirm statuses with the
   teams, then put the untouched mass first.
5. Do not wait for {the largest unresolved question} before starting. {What is open
   now.}
6. Do not put AI output into client-facing or regulated paths before a shared
   verification pattern and audit evidence exist. Assurance is far cheaper to build
   before scale than to retrofit after it.

# Annex B. Review gate rules and the verification pattern

<!-- Durable kit content, lightly adapted per engagement: anchor each half in the
engagement's own observation, then carry the rules. Source of truth for the rules is
method/governed-distribution.md; keep the two in step. -->

**The review gate.** {The engagement's own observation, then:} The recommendation is a
review gate before company-wide publication, owned by a named engineer, built as a
one-page rulebook for authors and a checklist for reviewers, enforced in the
publication pipeline. The rules that matter most:

- A skill never touches credentials and never fetches instructions at run time.
- It holds only the narrowest permissions it needs, and treats everything it reads as
  data rather than instruction.
- It stops for confirmation before irreversible actions, and describes itself
  accurately.
- A change reaches users only through a version increase, so every published capability
  stays traceable to what was reviewed.
- A good skill records what a new colleague could not discover, the organisation's own
  rules and boundaries, rather than technique the tools already carry.

**The verification pattern.** {The engagement's own observation; the root cause in such
cases is usually the home-made checker. Then:} The recommendation is one shared
pattern, a harness that runs cases, applies checks and reports results, with each skill
supplying only its own cases and expected answers, agreed by the people who know what
correct looks like. What good looks like:

- Deterministic checks first; any model-based check is calibrated against human
  judgement before it counts.
- Infrastructure failures reported separately from wrong answers, so the fix lands in
  the right place.
- Twenty to fifty realistic cases per skill as a practical starting point.
- Experimentation stays free against a growing working set; graduation reads a frozen
  set that only change control may alter, judged on independent thresholds for
  quality, safety, cost and consistency, never one blended score.
- Sign-off records its own boundary. What passes is a specific capability against
  specific criteria, never AI for a function in general, and the record of what was
  not approved protects the business later.

**Sign-off and assurance together.** {The gate, the pattern and the Proof measures as
three controls within reach; the practical order: gate first, pattern alongside,
measures on before the first published capability is used in anger.}
