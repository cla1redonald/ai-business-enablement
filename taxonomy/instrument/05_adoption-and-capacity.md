# Enabler 5: Adoption and capacity

*Can the organisation use this, and sustain it?* Four areas. This is where most
programmes of this kind succeed or quietly stop, and the areas run from the individual
(17) through the collective (18, 19) to the structural (20). Ownership is usually the
gap the others depend on.

## 17. Individual capability

**Definition.** How deeply individuals actually use AI: the distance between chatting
and delegating. The recurring ceiling: capable daily users working one request at a
time, moving outputs by hand to the next step, who would not have known to ask for what
sits one step further on. A change of tool is not a change of practice; migrated habits
(saved prompts becoming projects) are sensible and are not an advance.

**What to look for.** Breadth (which functions, which seniority; hands-on leadership
does more for adoption than sponsorship ever does). Depth: what share of users do
anything beyond chat and projects (skills, delegation, scheduled work). The
self-described "fairly good" user examined closely, who is usually the fair
representative of the business, not the outlier. Reactions to demonstrations of the next
step: "teach me step by step" means shown something wanted but not actionable; "no time
to learn it" is the capacity finding arriving from a different direction.

**How to surface it.** "Walk me through how you used AI yesterday, step by step."
"What is the most you have delegated to it end to end?" "What would you not have known
to ask for?" Watch a real session where possible; self-report inflates.

**Rating anchors.**
- In place: a material share of users working beyond chat: delegating multi-step work,
  using or building skills, with the practice spreading between peers.
- Partial: adoption genuine and broad, and the ceiling consistent: chat and projects,
  outputs moved by hand.
- Gap: usage thin, concentrated in a few enthusiasts, or performative.
- Blocked: rarely applicable.

**Sequencing note.** The gap between chatting and delegating is one concept wide, and
that concept is the whole skill. It is a training problem, not a capability problem,
and not a one-hour problem: demonstrations show what is possible; only step-by-step
teaching in protected time gets people doing it. The supplier's own self-serve courses
usually cover the basics without anything needing to be written.

## 18. Shared practice and training

**Definition.** Whether ways of working are shared and taught rather than individual:
standard practice, structured training, and time actually protected to learn. Duration
matters: adoption that has run person-by-person for six months or more is settled habit,
and habits are harder to redirect than beginnings.

**What to look for.** Any standard way of working or shared best practice. Structured
company-wide training versus optional drop-ins (optional one-hour sessions are good at
showing what is possible and poor at getting people doing it; when they have generated
requests for proper teaching, they have done their job and reached their limit).
Whether learning time exists anywhere, and whether the teams with the most to gain have
the least of it.

**How to surface it.** "Where would a team look for how this organisation uses AI
well?" "What training has actually run, and who attended?" "When did anyone last have
protected hours to learn a tool?"

**Rating anchors.**
- In place: shared practice documented and taught, training structured, learning time
  protected and used.
- Partial: real ad-hoc activity (drop-ins, champions, a few teams with habits) with no
  standard way of working and no protected time.
- Gap: nothing shared; every user self-taught in isolation.
- Blocked: rarely applicable.

**Sequencing note.** Replace the drop-in format once it has done its job. A dedicated
half day or day built around a real problem, hackathon-style with something finished at
the end, moves more people past the ceiling than further optional hours. The constraint
is not material; it is permission to spend the time.

## 19. Distribution and reuse

**Definition.** Whether there is a governed route from one person's working build to
something the whole company can use: an internal marketplace or equivalent, with review
in front of publication. Without it, finished tools strand with no route to users, and
every team rebuilds what a neighbour already has. This is the route by which anything
one person makes reaches everyone else, so it raises the return on every other
investment; little else has that property.

**What to look for.** Finished builds that cannot be rolled out (a polished internal
tool with no publication route is the tell). Whether a marketplace, repository or hub
exists or is being built, and who owns it. What the review checks: malicious behaviour
is the usual first check; licence and provenance need adding before third-party
submissions arrive; executable code inside skills makes the question live rather than
theoretical. Whether distribution preserves ownership (a shared link that leaves the
skill where it is maintained beats moving it).

**How to surface it.** "Show me something useful someone built. Who else can use it
today?" "What stands between a finished skill and the whole company having it?" "What
does the review actually check?"

**Rating anchors.**
- In place: a governed route live and used: review gate in front, named owner,
  versioned publication, licence and provenance checked.
- Partial: the route part-built or newly live with its controls still embryonic; or
  distribution works technically and review is a sanity check only.
- Gap: no route; working builds strand with their builders. (Mark "gap, closing now"
  when a build is genuinely under way with an owner.)
- Blocked: rarely applicable.

**Sequencing note.** Governed distribution is usually the only item that is both high
impact and low effort, and it is often already part-built. Design the licence check and
the read-only connector default in while the route is being built rather than
retrofitting either.

## 20. Ownership and capacity

**Definition.** Whether anyone owns making AI work, with the mandate, seniority and
protected time to do it, and whether a forum exists where AI decisions get taken.
Naming an owner in writing is necessary and not sufficient: an owner without a mandate
produces another item on somebody's backlog. Where no forum exists, decisions do not
get taken; they get deferred, and the same will happen to an assessment's
recommendations unless it changes.

**What to look for.** Who the policy names, and what capacity that person actually has
(direct observation: watch the support channel, count the team, listen for "living in
the backlog"). The structural pattern: where the CTO role points outward at product and
market, internal IT, security and data ownership falls by default to whoever is
nearest, producing a remit that has quietly grown past its job description. Whether the
largest decisions in the assessment have any home at executive level. Whether any
non-AI work in flight (device management, a migration) is quietly consuming the
capacity everything else depends on: the fastest route to capacity is often finishing
a piece of work that has nothing to do with AI. Items already stalled that nobody
tracks.

**How to surface it.** "Who owns making AI work here, and what else do they own?"
"Where does an AI decision get taken? Name the forum." "What is stalled right now, and
who would notice if one more thing joined it?"

**Rating anchors.**
- In place: a named owner with mandate, seniority and protected time; a forum that
  takes AI decisions; the major enablers tracked to completion.
- Partial: an owner named in writing and structurally unable to carry the remit; or a
  sponsoring executive without an operating owner beneath.
- Gap: nobody owns it, no forum exists, and the largest decisions have no home. This
  gap commonly explains why the largest unlock elsewhere in the assessment is
  undecided; draw that line explicitly.
- Blocked: rarely applicable.

**Sequencing note.** The remedy is the cheapest work in the report and the first: one
person accountable for the major enablers and the largest value drivers (not for every
initiative, which is neither realistic nor desirable), named by an executive who also
protects the time. Three routes work: hire deliberately into an open role, create the
role internally, or bring in temporary support with the exit condition agreed at the
start so temporary does not quietly become permanent. The test for any of them is
whether the knowledge ends up in-house. What the organisations that stalled had in
common is that they made no choice at all.
