# The session kit

These are the reusable formats for every session in the engagement that the client takes
part in: the kickoff messages, the discovery call, the departmental session and the
series of drop-ins. Anything in {braces} is a slot to fill, and the structures around
the slots come from the kit.

## 1. Kickoff messages

**The channel intro**, posted after the sponsor has introduced you. It covers these
points, in this order.

1. Thanks, and one human line. That can include an honest request for help with
   scheduling while access is being sorted out.
2. The session ask. Ask for an hour with each named lead, "optionally with a few of your
   team", because bringing a couple of people who do the work every day makes for a
   better hour than just the two of you. Include a calendar link. Say something like
   "I'd far rather do something rough and ready this week with two of you than wait a
   fortnight for the full team. Getting started matters more than getting everyone in
   the room at this stage."
3. The think-ahead list. It should take ten minutes and is not homework. Ask them to
   think about these things.
   - A typical week, and what each recurring task takes up.
   - Where work comes from and where it goes next, and which handoffs hurt.
   - The one big bottleneck.
   - What they have already tried with AI, whether it is working, abandoned or
     half-built ("the abandoned ones are often the most useful to me").
   - The tools they actually use, including anything nobody formally signed off.
4. The doc ask. Ask them to send anything that already exists. "Rough is fine, out of
   date is fine. I'd much rather read your messy real thing than a tidy version made for
   me."
5. The drop-in trailer. Say the first one is this week, as a lunch and learn. Be open
   that it has two purposes. They get quick wins, and you learn the landscape and find
   the enabling problems. Invite anyone who wants to come.

**The per-lead DM**, adapted for each person. Ask them to bring a couple of their team,
and to send what already exists. That might be how work flows, the tools they pay for,
what they have tried with AI, painful handoffs, and recurring reports with what each one
takes. "Don't make anything new for me. Send what exists."

**The prep table**, which stays private. It has one row per lead, with their area and
what you are listening for, plus a list of themes that cut across teams to test in every
session. Those themes are findability, document creation, shared context versus
individual tricks, fear about permissions, and measuring output rather than tokens.

## 2. The discovery call (book an hour)

Run it the same way every time. Open with the framing promise, "this is discovery, not
assessment, and nothing goes back as a scorecard", and ask to record ("purely so I'm
listening rather than typing").

| Minutes | Move |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Open with the framing and ask for consent to record |
| 5 to 15 | "Talk me through a normal week. Not the org chart version, the actual version." Interrupt only to pin a number, such as how often, how long or how many |
| 15 to 30 | "Which two things eat the most time?" Work through the probe list on each. This is where the value stream map comes from |
| 30 to 40 | Handoffs and waiting. Where does your work stop and someone else's start? What are you waiting on right now? What state does work arrive in? |
| 40 to 50 | What has been tried, whether working, half-built or abandoned. What made you stop? Has anything felt risky, or made you unsure whether you were allowed? |
| 50 to 55 | Close. Ask for the one thing they would unblock, who else you should talk to (especially the people doing the work every day), and anything they can send afterwards |
| 55 to 60 | Slack. Nothing is planned here, so a call that runs over still ends on time |

**The probe list**, for each heavy process. Ask how often it happens and how long it
takes each time. Ask where the input comes from and what state it arrives in, and **what
you do when it arrives wrong or incomplete**, because rework stays invisible unless you
ask about it. Ask who has to see it before it is finished, what they are waiting on and
for how long, and what happens if it is late. Ask how much of it is the same every time,
and which part they would happily never do again.

**Three rules in the room.** First, do not solve anything during the call. Note it and
move on, and if something would genuinely take five minutes, offer it at the very end.
Second, chase the boring work, such as chasing people, formatting, reconciling, copying
between systems and checking someone else's work, because that is where the hours go.
Third, if a lead answers for their team twice, go to the team directly. The difference
between the two accounts is usually the finding.

**Capture straight after each call.** Take ten minutes before the next one starts.

```
SESSION: [who] · [date]
TWO PROCESSES: name, frequency, duration, people, where it stalls
HANDOFF PAINS:
NUMBERS I CAN QUOTE:
VERBATIM (their words, especially anything starting "we always have to"):
TOOLS NAMED I DIDN'T KNOW ABOUT:
TRIED AND ABANDONED, AND WHY:
CONTRADICTS WHAT THE SPONSOR TOLD ME:
NEXT PERSON TO TALK TO:
```

The contradictions box matters. Where a department's account differs from the picture
the sponsor gave you, that difference is a finding in its own right.

## 3. The departmental session

This uses the discovery call format, with the lead plus two or three people who do the
work every day, and it concentrates on one function's heavy processes. Aim the questions
at the people doing the work rather than at the lead. Keep a one-page running order on
screen, and keep the detailed context on each person in the private prep table.

## 4. The drop-in series

The drop-ins are company-wide, optional, run like a lunch and learn, last roughly 45 to
60 minutes and are recorded. They have two purposes, and you say so openly. The room
gets quick wins, and the consultant learns the landscape and flushes out enabling
problems. Whatever fails live in a drop-in is evidence about readiness.

**How the series runs.** Session 1 is the taster. It has a short teach, then one
follow-along that everyone does on their own machine, then the poll that sets the menu
for the following weeks. Each later session covers a single topic, chosen by the room's
vote, pitched one gear beyond where the audience placed itself. Keep one topic back as
offered rather than voted, usually the session on governance or regulated data, because
the room under-votes the thing it most needs. Sequence the topics from context, to
capability, to composition. In practice that means who you are, then one repeated task
turned into a skill, then skills chained into a system that runs on its own.

**The polls**, run live in session 1 and repeated later to show movement.

1. *Focus.* Ask "where should I focus over the next month?" as free text or an option
   vote, and cluster the answers into the menu.
2. *The four gears.* Ask "where is your own team on this line: think with it, work with
   it, act agentically, built in?" Everyone can place themselves in thirty seconds. Read
   where the mass of the room sits, then **move the mass one gear, not two**, by picking
   the topic one gear beyond that.
3. *The blind spots*, for senior and technical people. Ask "what should I look at that I
   wouldn't think to ask?" This is not a topic vote, so use it to steer the enabling
   stream.

**Session design rules.**

- Give each session one outcome, stated on the first slide, and repeat one lesson three
  times. For the skills session the lesson is "fix the system, not the sentence".
- Make every session stand alone. Start with a five-minute recap, and never say "you
  should have been here last week".
- The follow-along is the point. It is a DO block of about 15 minutes where everyone
  builds the thing on their own machine, written for the most limited setup in the room
  (browser only, no admin rights), with success criteria on the slide. Never cut the
  hands-on part, and cut elsewhere if time runs short.
- Put templates on screen and in the channel, and post the recordings and steps
  afterwards, so the goal can be "two or three finished" rather than thirty.
- Prepare a table of what can go wrong live, such as a locked-down laptop, a missing
  feature or someone pasting real customer data, with a one-line response to each. Stop
  anyone pasting real data kindly and at once. The rules on handling data belong to the
  enabling stream.
- Prepare a bank of answers for the Q&A. A regulated room always asks the same questions
  ("can it read our files?", "is it safe to paste our stuff?", "where does this live?").
  Answer honestly without apologising, and send the detailed questions to the enabling
  stream.
- Bring the client's own IT into the governance part of the session. Have IT answer the
  approval question live, because the room believes it from them.
- Close every session by following up whatever was promised in the last one. Check the
  product the day before, because button names move, and never present something out of
  date as fact.
- Present from a 16:9 window, have someone watching the chat, test the login for the
  poll tool, and rehearse the live demo once. Protect the part where you show "what I do
  when it gets it wrong", because that is the most valuable part.

**The fictional sandbox.** Demos run in a company folder that is entirely fictional,
never in client or personal material. It holds a house-rules context file ("never invent
a number", "nothing posts without approval", "sensitive topics go to a human") and a few
skills. It also has one or two subagents, including an independent reviewer, and local
seed data so that every run can be repeated offline. Keep a pristine copy of the data
and restore it before presenting, because the append-only logs change on every rerun.
Script the guardrail moment on purpose. Push one story the reviewer must flag and one it
must fail, because watching the system refuse something is what convinces a regulated
room. Copy the folder out of any personal vault before you share your screen. The story
of the demo is that a folder turns into a system that runs itself. You meet the folder,
run the chain once, watch the guardrail catch something, approve a good one, and set it
running weekly.

## 5. Pairing for quick wins

Alongside the sessions, pair with the people who do the work, on one real task each.
Good candidates are the recurring report, the dashboard rebuilt by hand, or the upload
done by copy and paste. What you build stays with the team, and findings go into the
report only where they bear on a decision. The first assisted run of a real task, timed
against how long it takes by hand, is the cheapest evidence of value the engagement will
collect.
