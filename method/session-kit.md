# The session kit

The reusable formats for every client-facing session in the engagement: the kickoff
messages, the discovery call, the departmental session, and the drop-in series. Slots
are {braced}; the structures are kit.

## 1. Kickoff messages

**The channel intro** (posted after the sponsor's introduction). Structure, in order:

1. Thanks and one human line (including any honest ask for scheduling help while access
   is being sorted).
2. **The session ask**: an hour with each named lead, "optionally with a few of your
   team": bringing a couple of people who do the work daily makes a better hour than
   just the two of us. Calendar link. "I'd far rather do something rough and ready this
   week with two of you than wait a fortnight for the full team: momentum matters more
   than completeness at this stage."
3. **The think-ahead list** (ten minutes, not homework): a typical week and what each
   recurring thing eats; where work arrives from and goes next, and which handoffs
   hurt; the one big bottleneck; what you have already tried with AI, working,
   abandoned or half-built ("the abandoned ones are often the most useful to me"); the
   tools you actually use, including anything nobody formally signed off.
4. **The doc ask**: send anything that already exists. "Rough is fine, out of date is
   fine. I'd much rather read your messy real thing than a tidy version made for me."
5. **The drop-in trailer**: first one this week as a lunch and learn; dual purpose,
   stated openly (you get quick wins, I learn the landscape and flush out enabling
   problems); invite extended to anyone.

**The per-lead DM** (adapt per person): bring a couple of your team; send what exists
(how work flows, tools paid for, AI tried, painful handoffs, recurring reports and what
each takes). "Don't make anything new for me. Send what exists."

**The prep table** (private): one row per lead: area, what I'm listening for, plus a
cross-cutting theme list to test in every session (findability, document creation,
shared context versus per-person tricks, permissions fear, measuring output not
tokens).

## 2. The discovery call (55 minutes)

Same shape every time. Open with the framing promise: "this is discovery, not
assessment; nothing goes back as a scorecard", and the recording ask ("purely so I'm
listening rather than typing").

| Minutes | Move |
|---|---|
| 0 to 3 | Open: framing, recording consent |
| 3 to 12 | "Talk me through a normal week. Not the org chart version, the actual version." Interrupt only to pin a number: how often, how long, how many |
| 12 to 32 | "Which two things eat the most time?" Work the probe list on each; this is where the value stream map comes from |
| 32 to 42 | Handoffs and waiting: where does your work stop and someone else's start; what are you waiting on right now; what state does work arrive in |
| 42 to 50 | What's been tried: working, half-built or abandoned; what made you stop; has anything felt risky or made you unsure whether you were allowed |
| 50 to 55 | Close: the one thing you would unblock; who else should I talk to (especially the people doing the work daily); anything you can send me afterwards |

**The probe list** (per heavy process): how often and how long each time; where the
input comes from and what state it arrives in; **what you do when it arrives wrong or
incomplete** (rework stays invisible unless asked); who has to see it before it is
finished; what you are waiting on and for how long; what happens if it is late; how
much is the same every time; which part you would happily never do again.

**Three rules in the room.** Don't solve anything in the room (note it, move on;
anything genuinely five minutes, offer at the very end). Chase the boring stuff:
chasing, formatting, reconciling, copying between systems, checking someone else's
work; that is where the hours are. If a lead answers for their team twice, go direct:
the gap between the two accounts is usually the finding.

**Capture, straight after each call** (ten minutes, before the next one):

```
SESSION: [who] · [date]
TWO PROCESSES: name, frequency, duration, people, where it stalls
HANDOFF PAINS:
NUMBERS I CAN QUOTE:
VERBATIM (their words, especially anything starting "we always have to"):
TOOLS NAMED I DIDN'T KNOW ABOUT:
TRIED AND ABANDONED, AND WHY:
CONTRADICTS WHAT THE SPONSOR TOLD ME:
NEXT PERSON TO TALK TO:
```

The contradictions box matters: where a department's account differs from the
sponsor's verbal picture, that difference is itself a finding.

## 3. The departmental session

The discovery call format, with the lead plus two or three people who do the work
daily, aimed at one function's heavy processes. Aim the questions at the doers, not
the lead. A one-page running order stays on screen; deep per-person context lives in
the private prep table.

## 4. The drop-in series

Company-wide, optional, lunch-and-learn shaped, roughly 45 to 60 minutes, recorded.
Dual purpose stated openly: the room gets quick wins; the consultant learns the
landscape and flushes out enabling problems (what fails live in a drop-in is readiness
evidence).

**The shape of the series.** Session 1 is the taster: a short teach, one follow-along
everyone does on their own seat, then the menu poll for the following weeks. The
following sessions are single-topic, voted by the room, one gear beyond where the
audience placed itself. Reserve one topic as offered rather than voted (the governance
or regulated-data session; the room under-votes what it most needs). Sequence logic:
context, then capability, then composition (who you are; one repeated task as a skill;
skills chained into a system that runs itself).

**The poll instruments** (run live in session 1, repeat later to show movement):

1. *Focus*: "where should I focus over the next month?" Free-text or option vote;
   cluster the answers into the menu.
2. *The four gears*: "where is your own team on this line: think with it, work with
   it, act agentically, built in?" Everyone can self-place in thirty seconds. Read the
   mass, then **move the mass one gear, not two**: pick the topic one gear beyond the
   bunch.
3. *The blind spots* (senior and technical): "what should I look at that I wouldn't
   think to ask?" Not a topic vote; steer for the enabling stream.

**Session design rules.**

- One outcome per session, stated on the first slide, and one lesson repeated three
  times ("fix the system, not the sentence" for the skills session).
- Standalone rule: five-minute recap at the top, never "you should have been here
  last week".
- The follow-along is the point: a 12-minute DO block where everyone builds the thing
  on their own seat, written for the most constrained seat in the room (browser only,
  no admin rights). Success criteria on the slide. Never cut the hands-on to save
  time; cut a demo instead.
- Templates on screen and in the channel; recordings and steps posted afterwards so
  the goal can be "two or three finished, not thirty".
- Prepare the failure-mode table: for each thing that can go wrong live (a locked-down
  laptop, a missing feature, someone pasting real customer data), the one-line
  response. Stop real-data pastes kindly and immediately; the data-handling rules are
  the enabling stream's job.
- Prepare the Q&A bank; a regulated room always asks the same questions ("can it read
  our files?", "is it safe to paste our stuff?", "where does this live?"). Answer
  honestly, not apologetically, and route the depth to the enabling stream.
- Co-present the governance moment with the client's own IT: the strongest ninety
  seconds of a drop-in is IT answering the approval question live.
- Close every session by opening the loops promised in the last one, and verify
  product surfaces the day before (button names move; never present stale as fact).
- Present from a 16:9 window, have a chat-watcher, test the poll tool login, rehearse
  the live demo once and protect the "what I do when it gets it wrong" beat: that is
  the valuable part.

**The fictional sandbox pattern.** Demos run in a fully fictional company folder,
never client or personal material: a house-rules context file ("never invent a
number", "nothing posts without approval", "sensitive topics go to a human"), a few
skills, one or two subagents including an independent reviewer, and local seed data so
every run is reproducible and offline. Keep a pristine copy of the data and restore it
before presenting (append-only logs change reruns). Script the guardrail moment
deliberately: push a story the reviewer must flag and one it must fail, because the
governed refusal is what sells the system to a regulated room. Copy the folder out of
any personal vault before screen-sharing. The narrative arc in one sentence: **a
folder becomes a system that runs itself** (meet the folder, run the chain once, watch
the guardrail catch something, approve a good one, set it running weekly).

## 5. Pairing for quick wins

Alongside the sessions, pair with the people who do the work on one real task each
(the recurring report, the dashboard rebuilt by hand, the upload done by copy and
paste). Deliverables stay with the team; findings fold into the report only where they
bear on a decision. The first assisted run of a real task, timed against its manual
baseline, is the cheapest value evidence the engagement will collect.
