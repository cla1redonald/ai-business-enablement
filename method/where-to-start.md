# The where-to-start kit

Everything that guides sequencing, as one named bundle. This is the "identify their top
opportunities, fast" half of the scenario, and it runs as a facilitated session pack
straight out of the kit. The client keeps it: repeatable every quarter and at every
reorganisation.

## The two decisions (before any work)

**Decision A.** Name one accountable owner for the enabler programme, with time
protected by a named executive who also protects learning time for the stretched teams.
It costs little and is the cheapest test of whether the organisation means to move.

**Decision B.** Confirm the outcome the work serves: growth-led or cost-led. A
growth-led reading points the first portfolio at the commercial streams; a cost-led
reading points it at the internal reporting and copy-paste assembly work. The two
readings select different starting sets from the same discovery, so the choice comes
before the set does.

## The wave rule

A wave is a group of moves that wait on nothing outside the group, so wave one can
start the day the decision is made. Wave one is typically the review gate and
verification pattern, connector scoping, the cheapest data connections, and the
measurement moves. Wave two is typically the identity and permission rollout. Wave
three builds on both: the data platform, agent hosting, widened review capacity.

**Never run an enabler bare.** An enabler pays back only when a business outcome is
delivered across it, and the design details only surface under a real case, so every
wave pairs groundwork with a visible win.

## The quick-win rule

The quick win is the stream with the most quantified evidence that waits on nothing.
Shape it as a bounded test, bring any in-flight tool trial on that stream to a decision
as part of it, and run it on capability that has passed the verification pattern, so it
proves an enabler as it goes. If the owning team is under pressure, reshape the test to
near-zero cost for them; asking what the client must protect is part of sequencing.

## The gating counts

An opportunity is gated when it waits on at least one enabling gap, open when it waits
on none. Gated is not lesser: a gated route's first step is the gate. Prefer gate moves
that release many opportunities; the opportunities-waiting count per gap is what turns
the diagnosis into a sequence.

## The six questions (the choosing session)

Asked in order, they take a leadership team from the full discovery to a defensible
starting set in one working session, choosing among routes. The enablers are not on the
menu: they proceed in wave order regardless, and their gaps enter only as the gates
that decide whether a route is open.

1. **Where does unstarted value concentrate?** Rank functions by weighted mass, then
   ask each owning team what is genuinely already moving.
2. **Is the path open or gated?** Open routes can start now; gated routes are not
   lesser, their first step is the gate.
3. **If gated, what does moving the gate release?** Prefer gates that release many
   opportunities.
4. **How strong is the evidence underneath?** Deep evidence supports commitment; thin
   evidence supports a verification step before a build.
5. **What does it cost to try?** Grade effort route by route and use it to order the
   starting set. Effort decides the sequence and should never decide worth: a hard
   route at the top of the discovery still outranks an easy one at the bottom.
6. **What does the route assume, and who owns testing it?** State the assumption, name
   an owner, and test it before funding the work.

**The column map**: each question maps to a workbook column so the session runs on the
client's own data; the mapping lives in the report's annex E and the workbook's Read
me, generated from one source. Before the session, the owning teams re-confirm what is
moving, because the evidence describes a stated period and no delivery statuses are
recorded. Expected output: one gate move, a few open-route starts where untouched value
concentrates, and one measurement move so results can be proven. Re-run quarterly, at
any change of structure or budget, and whenever an enabling gap closes.

## The don'ts

1. **Do not build a weighted composite score** to rank the opportunities. The weights
   would be invented rather than evidenced, and the argument shifts from the work to
   the scoring formula. (The don't execs remember; say it early.)
2. **Do not plan or grade effort for a whole stream at once.** A single grade at that
   level means little. Commit route by route.
3. **Do not ask the evidence to justify role-level decisions.** Gathered under a
   productivity scope, it describes friction in processes and says nothing about
   whether any role is needed.
4. **Do not add new investment where work is already moving.** Confirm statuses with
   the teams, then put the untouched mass first.
5. **Do not wait for the largest unresolved question before starting.** Name what is
   open now; once the two decisions are made, wave one needs nothing more than the
   will to run it.
6. **Do not put AI output into client-facing or regulated paths** before a shared
   verification pattern and audit evidence exist. Assurance is far cheaper to build
   before scale than to retrofit after it.

## Reading a map

Read a value stream map by its waits and its pain, not its busiest steps. The longest
waits usually hold the time, and the worst pain marks the routes worth testing first.
A route is a path through the map; routes are what the six questions choose between.
