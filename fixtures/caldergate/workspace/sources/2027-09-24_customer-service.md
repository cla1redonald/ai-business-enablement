S1: Rob, take me through yesterday. What did you actually spend it doing?

S3: Where's-my-shipment, mostly. We take about 60 status queries a day across the team. Each one means Loadstone for the booking, the carrier's portal for the tracking, and usually the email thread for anything odd. Three places for one answer, and the customer's on hold while I swivel.

S1: How long does one take?

S3: A clean one, five minutes. One with a problem in it, twenty, thirty, because then I'm into the escalation dance.

S2: Which is its own thing. If it needs operations, Rob types the story into an email or rings Aileen's desk, and whatever he wrote gets typed again into Loadstone notes on their side. The customer's told "we're looking into it" and we genuinely are, but nobody could show you where any given escalation is.

S1: What question do you answer most often, and where does the answer live?

S3: Cut-off times. What's the latest a consignment can reach the depot and still make Friday's sailing. The answer lives in a spreadsheet on Filestead that operations update, except when they update it late.

S1: Do you track anything on customers going quiet, churn risk, that kind of signal?

S2: Nothing. We know it when we feel it. There's no number that would tell you an account's gone cold until the volume's already gone.

S1: What have you tried with AI?

S2: We set up a Verrin project to answer the common questions, loaded it with the FAQ doc and the cut-off spreadsheet. It was good until it was wrong: it gave a customer the previous season's cut-off, confidently, and the shipment missed the sailing. That was the end of that, nobody trusted it after.

S1: I bet the cut-off table it was reading had gone stale rather than the answers being made up.

S2: Could be. Either way we had no way of checking it was right before it went out, and that's what killed it. If someone could show me it giving right answers reliably, the team would use it tomorrow. The demand's all still there, the queries haven't gone anywhere.
